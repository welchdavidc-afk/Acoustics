How to EQ a room, step by step

Equalisation (EQ) trims a system's frequency response so the room and speakers sound more even. It can reduce peaks and ringing, but it cannot fix dips caused by cancellation, or a room that is too reverberant. Keep the volume low while setting up and protect your hearing.

  1. Prepare the room and system. Put the speakers where they will be used, furnish the room as normal, close doors and switch off fans and other noise you can control. Set every EQ on the system to flat (0 dB) and turn off any automatic room correction while you measure.
  2. Prepare the microphone. Use the best microphone you have. On the Setup tab do the general calibration, and if you have a reference meter or a calibration file, use the advanced frequency calibration. Mount the phone still at ear height at the main listening position (about 1.2 m for seated listeners), away from walls and furniture, pointing at the speakers.
  3. Choose the test signal. Play pink noise (equal energy in every octave) through the system. The Tone generator on the Setup tab can make it, or use a pink noise track. Set a level that is clearly measurable, about 75 to 85 dB(A) at the listening position and at least 15 dB above the room's background noise. If the Live page warns of clipping, turn the level down.
  4. Set up the analyser. On the Setup tab set Frequency weighting to None (flat) and Time weighting to Slow. On the Live page choose the bar style (1/3-octave) and switch on Peak levels to see the highest level reached at each frequency. A flat system measured with pink noise shows bars of about the same height.
  5. Read the response. Let the bars settle for 10 to 20 seconds. Note narrow peaks (more than about 4 to 6 dB above their neighbours), broad bumps and dips, and the overall slope from bass to treble. Many people like a gentle fall in level toward the highest frequencies. Pick a target shape and stay with it.
  6. Check more than one position. Repeat at several seats and compare. EQ should fix problems that show up at most positions. A dip seen at only one position is usually cancellation, so try moving the speaker or listener instead of boosting it.
  7. Find the exact frequencies. Turn on Dominant tones, or use Touch cursor, to read the frequency and level of each peak. Booms and ringing below about 300 Hz are often room modes. If microphones are in use, turn on Feedback detect and raise the gain slowly until the system just starts to ring. The frequencies marked FB are the first to cut.
  8. Decide what to cut. Cut peaks and do not boost dips: boosting wastes amplifier headroom and cannot fix cancellation. Use a narrow cut (high Q, about 5 to 10) for a narrow peak and a wide cut (low Q, about 1 to 2) or a shelf for a broad bump. Start with about half the height of the peak (3 dB for a 6 dB peak) and rarely cut more than 6 to 9 dB at one frequency.
  9. Change one thing at a time. On the system's graphic or parametric EQ, set a filter at the frequency you found, reduce its gain and watch the graph. Press the reset icon above MAX so the peak levels start fresh after each change. Stop when the peaks sit within about 3 dB of your target.
  10. Check the room itself. EQ changes the response, not the reverberation. Use the Impulse tab to measure reverberation time and background noise against your target (for example AS/NZS 2107). If the reverberation time is too long, add absorption. EQ will not fix it.
  11. Listen and confirm. Play speech and music you know well at normal levels. If it sounds thin or harsh you have probably cut too much, so back off a little. Write down the final settings.
  12. Record the result. Fill in the Report tab and share the PDF, noting the room state and the EQ settings in the notes. Check again after changes to furniture, audience size or equipment, because a full room absorbs more than an empty one.

A phone microphone is weak below about 50 Hz and can be uneven at high frequencies, so treat these measurements as a guide. For critical work use a calibrated measurement microphone with the advanced frequency calibration. The numbers in these steps (levels, Q values, cut sizes) are common practice, not requirements of a standard.

Appearance

Measurement settings

Spectrum resolution is how finely the Live graph, peaks and waterfall divide the frequency range. Narrower bands show more detail and read lower for broad noise, while steady tones read the same.

Display response is how much sound the spectrum looks at each time. Fast shows changes sooner but has less detail at low frequencies, and tone, fundamental and feedback detection then start from about 150 Hz. Detailed shows the most low-frequency detail. Times assume a 48 kHz microphone.

These apply to the Live page. The Impulse and Report results always use dB(A) for background noise.

Calibration

Best: place an acoustic calibrator (1 kHz, usually 94 dB) on the mic, wait 5 seconds, then press Calibrate. Without one, compare against a trusted meter in steady noise and adjust the offset. The frequency and time weighting settings do not matter here: calibration uses the flat (unweighted) level, and A, C and flat weighting are all 0 dB at 1 kHz anyway. If your calibrator is not at 1 kHz, enter its unweighted level.

Uncalibrated

Calibration is stored for this microphone on this browser. Phone microphones typically bottom out around 30 to 35 dB(A) from their own noise, and distort above roughly 115 to 120 dB.

Advanced: frequency calibration

The general calibration above sets one offset for the whole microphone and is all most users need. Use this only if you can check the microphone against a reference meter or have a calibration file for it. Corrections are relative to 1 kHz, apply to the meter, spectrum, noise test and recordings, are saved per microphone, and add about 20 ms of delay while on.

To set a frequency: play a steady tone at that frequency from a speaker at a fixed spot, read its level on a reference meter, type that in Reference dB and press Set. Use a tone at least 20 dB above the room noise. The 1 kHz row adjusts the general offset instead.

Tone generator

Plays a steady tone or pink noise for calibration and room EQ. Start with the phone volume low, because a tone is much louder than it sounds. To calibrate, play it from a speaker (this phone's, or another phone or tablet running this app) and compare your microphone with a reference meter in the same spot. Feedback detect may flag the tone, which is expected.

About this version

Prototype. Uses the Web Audio API in Chrome. A and C weighting are digital filters (bilinear transform, matched at 1 kHz) so accuracy above about 10 kHz is approximate. This is not a Class 1 or Class 2 sound level meter (IEC 61672).
